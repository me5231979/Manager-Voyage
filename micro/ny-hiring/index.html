    Hiring in New York.

    A ten-minute module for anyone who hires for a role based in New York. Four rules change how you interview and make an offer: background checks run in two phases, the offer is conditional until they finish, salary history is off the table, and every candidate gets the same fair interview. Your recruiter runs the process. This module makes sure you never get ahead of it.

    Narration is on. Every page is read as it turns. Tap Auto to turn it off, or Listen to replay a page.

    By the end, you will be able to
    • Sequence the two background check phases and the conditional offer between them
    • Extend a verbal offer using the approved conditional language, word for word
    • Recognize the salary questions you cannot ask, and what you can ask instead
    • Run a structured interview: the same questions and a scorecard for every candidate
    • Return interview feedback to your recruiter within the agreed timeline

    Topic 1 Why the rules exist · Next: the sequence

    Why it matters

    Fair hiring is the law in New York.

    What you will learnThe four rules, where each comes from, and what they protect.

    Why it mattersGetting the order wrong, or one sentence in an offer, creates legal risk for you and for Vanderbilt, and a poor experience for a candidate who did nothing wrong.

    Judge the job, not the past.

    New York City’s Fair Chance Act, as amended in 2021, puts criminal history at the end of the process: only after a conditional offer, and only weighed against the duties of the role. State law also keeps arrests that did not lead to a conviction, and sealed records, out of hiring altogether.

    NYC Commission on Human Rights, Fair Chance Act FAQ (2021); NY Executive Law 296(16); NY Clean Slate Act (2024).
    Pay for the role, not the history.

    Since 2020, New York employers may not ask about or rely on a candidate’s current or prior pay. Since 2023, every posting carries the pay range. Together they mean an offer is built from the job, the market, and internal equity.

    NY Labor Law 194-a and 194-b.
    The fair interview is the better interview.

    Asking every candidate the same job-related questions and scoring them against a rubric predicts performance better than an open conversation, and narrows the gaps between groups of candidates. The law and the research point the same way.

    Schmidt and Hunter (1998), Psychological Bulletin; U.S. OPM, Structured Interviews guide.

    Topic 2 The sequence · Next: the conditional offer

    The sequence

    Two phases, one conditional offer between them.

    What you will learnWhat happens before any offer, what a conditional offer is, and what may only happen after it.

    Why it mattersThe order is the compliance. A verbal offer one day early, or a criminal check one day early, is the violation.

    Phase 1 · Before any offerNon-criminal checks.
    • References, and employment and education verification
    • Run by Recruiting; your recruiter confirms when Phase 1 is complete
    • No verbal or written offer of any kind until then
    then
    The conditional offerVerbal from you, written from your recruiter.
    • After Phase 1 is complete and you and your recruiter agree to move forward
    • You say the approved script, nothing more
    • Recruiting sends the written conditional offer
    then
    Phase 2 · After the written offerCriminal background check.
    • Begins only after the written conditional offer is issued
    • If something comes back, Recruiting runs the Fair Chance process: a written assessment, shared with the candidate, with at least five business days to respond
    • You never decide that alone

    Topic 3 The conditional offer · Next: salary history

    The verbal offer

    One script, said the same way every time.

    What you will learnThe approved conditional language, what each phrase does, and the sentences that undo it.

    Why it mattersAn offer that sounds final is treated as final. If a later check changes the outcome, the promise you made is the problem.

    Required verbal offer language
    “We’re excited to extend you a conditional offer, pending successful completion of background checks and final onboarding steps…”
    “Conditional”

    Tells the candidate, in the first sentence, that the offer can still change.

    “Pending background checks”

    Names what it depends on, so Phase 2 is expected, not a surprise.

    “Final onboarding steps”

    Covers the rest of the process without promising a date.

    Do not add to it. “The job is yours,” “it’s just a formality,” and “you can give notice” each turn a conditional offer into a promise.

    Try all three openings. The activity completes when you find the one that holds.

    Topic 4 Salary history · Next: a fair interview

    Salary history

    Ask about expectations, never about history.

    What you will learnWhat New York Labor Law 194-a forbids, what it allows, and what to do when a candidate volunteers a number.

    Why it mattersPay built on past pay carries every past inequity forward. Pay built on the role does not.

    You may not
    • Ask what a candidate earns now or earned before, directly or indirectly
    • Ask a reference, a former employer, or a recruiter for it
    • Use prior pay to decide whether to make an offer, or what to offer
    You may
    • Ask what salary the candidate expects for this role
    • Share the pay range for the role; it is in the posting already
    • Base the offer on the role, market data, and internal equity

    If a candidate volunteers their salary history: let it pass. No follow-up questions, and it does not go into the offer discussion or your notes.

    Topic 5 A fair interview · Next: your call

    Interviewing

    Same questions, a scorecard, and feedback on time.

    What you will learnThe four practices of a structured interview, and the questions that are never asked.

    Why it mattersStructured interviews predict performance better than conversations and narrow the gaps between groups. They also protect you: every candidate can say they were asked the same things.

    1. Decide before you meet anyone.

    With your recruiter, pick the four to six things the job really needs. Write one behavioral question for each (“Tell me about a time…”) and what a strong answer sounds like.

    2. Ask everyone the same questions.

    Same questions, same order, for every candidate. Probe with follow-ups on their answer, not with new questions.

    3. Score before you talk.

    Rate each answer against the rubric before the panel compares notes. Write what they said and did, not how they seemed.

    4. Feedback within the timeline.

    Send your scores and decision to your recruiter within the agreed timeline. Slow processes lose good candidates, and the recruiter cannot start Phase 1 without you.

    Never asked, in any form: age or graduation year, family plans or children, citizenship or origin (you may ask whether they are authorized to work in the United States), religion, disability or health, arrests that are not pending or any sealed record, credit, and salary history. If it is not about the job, it is not a question.
