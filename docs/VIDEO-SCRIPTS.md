# Foundation course videos: scripts and shot lists

Each video is instructional first (what the topic is, why it matters, the habits), then one short example drawn from the course scenario. B-roll spans dining, facilities, lab, library and grounds; no IT service desk. No clip is used in more than one video.

## basics

What a manager is. A manager is the person responsible for a team’s work, and for the people who do it. That is the whole change. Before, your job was your work. Now it is the team’s work, whether you run a kitchen, a lab, a library desk, or a grounds crew. Three things are new. You decide: what matters this week, who does what, what waits. You approve: timecards, time off, expenses. You are responsible for your team, and accuracy is key. Timesheets, payroll, and every approval must be right. And you are responsible for people: fair treatment, a safe place to speak up, a chance to grow. Three questions, every week. Does everyone know what to do this week? Is anyone stuck, or waiting on me? What is coming that the team should hear from me first? For example: a new dining manager keeps plating faster than anyone while her cooks wait for decisions only she can make. The moment she steps back, names the three things that matter today, and signs the timecards, lunch runs better, and she did not plate a tray. You do not have to know everything. Your manager, your Engagement Consultant, and your HCM are there. You do have to show up as the manager.

## framework

The four jobs of a manager. Every manager, in every part of Vanderbilt, does the same four jobs every week. Job one, get the work done: plan it, say what good looks like, check it before it is due, and fix the cause when it breaks. Job two, take care of your people: listen, grow them, thank them, trust them. Job three, make things better: explain the why, describe where the team is going, let people try, look back. Job four, connect your team to the rest of Vanderbilt: know the people your team depends on, watch for what is coming, speak up for your team. Nothing here is new work. It is the same work, sorted, and the assessment you took scores these same habits. The skill is noticing which job this week needs most. For example: a facilities supervisor starts Monday with four asks. A backlog of work orders. A new technician who wants to learn the boiler. A new work-order system. And Events asking for his crew on Friday. One list, four different jobs. Sorting it tells him where the week is heavy. This week: sort your own list into the four jobs.

## task

Job one: get the work done. When people are not sure what is expected, work comes in late or gets redone, and you find out on the due date. Four habits prevent that. Plan it: decide what matters, who owns it, and what gives. Say it: what is expected, by when, and what good looks like, out loud and in writing. Check it: look at the work before it is due, while there is still time to help. Fix it: when the same thing breaks twice, find the cause, not the person. And one habit to stop: doing the work yourself because it is faster. For example: a monthly report your team owns is due Friday. The person who builds it has not mentioned it in two weeks, and a director is asking. Building it yourself Thursday night is the old job. A fifteen-minute check-in on Wednesday, where the report stands and what she needs, is the manager’s job. This week: pick one person and confirm, out loud, what they own and by when.

## relations

Job two: take care of your people. People stay, and do their best work, for a manager who notices them. Four habits. Listen and help: notice pressure, move what you can, follow up. Grow them: ask where they want to go, then find the stretch. Thank them: specific, by name, that week. Great job everyone thanks nobody. Trust them: hand over the decision, not just the task. And one line every manager learns: when someone mentions health, family, or leave, you adjust the load, and the leave office decides eligibility. Send it the same day, through Origami, and never ask for a diagnosis. For example: a lab technician says her mother is in and out of the hospital and she might need time. Her manager moves the two deadlines she has this week, sends the request the same day, and tells her he did. A week later she is back, and the team greets her at the door. This week: thank one person for one specific thing.

## change

Job three: make things better. Vanderbilt is asking every team to move faster and improve, and that happens in your team, in small ways, every week. It is not a reorganization. It is four habits. Explain the why: the reason, in your own words, before the announcement lands. Describe where you are going: two sentences on what the team will be able to do next year that it cannot do now. Let people try: small, short trials of new ideas. Look back: ten minutes after big work. What worked, what did not, what changes. For example: central is replacing a library’s checkout system in six weeks, and the team has already said, here we go again. The manager explains the why at her own staff meeting before the campus email, writes down the hard questions to take back, lets a young staffer test a new cart routine for two weeks, and holds a ten-minute look-back after move-in weekend. The team still did not love the system. But they heard the why from her first, and two of them volunteered to test it early. This week: hold one ten-minute look-back.

## external

Job four: connect your team to the rest of Vanderbilt. Your team needs things it does not control: approvals, information, help from other offices, and a fair hearing when someone asks too much. New managers do this job least, because nobody told them it was part of the job. Three habits. Know the people your team depends on: your HCM, your Engagement Consultant, Finance, a peer manager. By name, before you need them. Watch for what is coming, so the team hears it from you first. Speak up for your team: facts, options, and a negotiated date, before you say yes to the weekend. For example: the week before Commencement, Events asks a grounds supervisor for his whole crew on Friday. He already knows the events coordinator by name. The schedule was on his wall calendar in March. At a picnic table with the printed hours, he names the conflict and offers two options. She picks one, nobody works the weekend, and on Commencement morning the chairs are in rows. This week: have coffee with one person your team depends on.
