Idea 1 · Priorities

=== Page 12 of 57 · Idea 1 · Priorities (page key ideas/2) ===
SETTING PRIORITIES · THE BIG ROCKS METHOD
STANDARD 01 · GET THE WORK DONE
Put first things first.
SAND FIRST
Email and small requests go in first. The report, the hire, the 1:1s spill over the top.
ROCKS FIRST
Three named priorities go in first. The small things fit in the gaps.

WHAT IT IS
Picture a jar. The rocks are the high-impact work: the report your team owns, the hire, your 1:1s. The sand is the quick, low-value wins: email, small requests. Sand first and the rocks never fit. Rocks first and the sand settles around them.

START THIS WEEK
Write your three big rocks before you open your inbox. Tell your team.

FOR YOU AS A MANAGER
When everything is urgent, nothing is. Setting the big rocks with your team on Monday is how you stop firefighting.

WHY IT WORKS, WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE, AND WHAT IT BRINGS

WHY ADOPT IT
Your calendar fills itself. Requests arrive faster than you can finish them, and the urgent ones are rarely the important ones. Without three named priorities, the loudest request decides for you.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE IN PRACTICE
Monday, before email: three lines on a notepad. Said aloud at the huddle, on the calendar by nine. A request lands Wednesday; you ask which rock it moves.

THE VALUE IT BRINGS
A team that knows this week’s three things wastes less, argues less, and finishes more.

Idea 2 · Clear expectations

=== Page 14 of 57 · Idea 2 · Clear expectations (page key ideas/4) ===
CLEAR EXPECTATIONS AND FEEDBACK · CLEAR IS KIND
STANDARD 01 · GET THE WORK DONE
Clear is kind. Unclear is unkind.

WHAT IT IS
Say what you expect, by when, and what good looks like. Give feedback close to the moment, in private, on the specific thing.

START THIS WEEK
Say one thing you have been hinting at. Privately, plainly, kindly.

FOR YOU AS A MANAGER
Most performance problems are expectation problems. Say what good looks like and by when, and most of them never start.

WHY IT WORKS, WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE, AND WHAT IT BRINGS

WHY ADOPT IT
Hinting feels kind and costs weeks. People guess, redo the work, and hear about the problem late, when it is bigger and harder to fix.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE IN PRACTICE
“The summary needs the decision on page one, under three hundred words, by Thursday at three. Here is why.” Said in the 1:1, written in the follow-up.

THE VALUE IT BRINGS
Fewer surprises at the deadline, fewer redo cycles, and a team that always knows where it stands.

Idea 3 · Psychological safety

=== Page 16 of 57 · Idea 3 · Psychological safety (page key ideas/6) ===
PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY
STANDARD 02 · TAKE CARE OF YOUR PEOPLE
People only speak up when it is safe to.

WHAT IT IS
A team’s shared belief that nobody is punished or embarrassed for a question, a mistake, or a disagreement. Not about being nice: about making the truth cheap to tell.

START THIS WEEK
When someone reports a problem, thank them first. Then solve it.

FOR YOU AS A MANAGER
People only tell you bad news early if it is safe to. The first time someone brings you a mistake decides whether they ever do it again.

WHY IT WORKS, WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE, AND WHAT IT BRINGS

WHY ADOPT IT
Late news is the most expensive kind. A team that fears the reaction hides mistakes until they are big, and stops asking questions before they become errors.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE IN PRACTICE
Bad news lands: “Thank you for telling me today. What do we know, and what do you need from me?” A basic question in the meeting gets a straight answer. You say “I got that wrong” out loud.

THE VALUE IT BRINGS
You hear about problems while they are small. The best teams report more mistakes, not fewer, because they catch them early.

Idea 4 · Purpose

=== Page 18 of 57 · Idea 4 · Purpose (page key ideas/8) ===
PURPOSE · START WITH WHY, TEAM FIRST
STANDARD 03 · MAKE THINGS BETTER
Explain the why. Take care of the people, and they take care of the work.

WHAT IT IS
People do their best work when they know why it matters, not just what to do. A team that feels looked after protects the work and each other.

START THIS WEEK
Explain the why behind one thing you have been asking for.

FOR YOU AS A MANAGER
Your team will do a hard thing for a reason. Explaining the why is the part of a change only you can do.

WHY IT WORKS, WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE, AND WHAT IT BRINGS

WHY ADOPT IT
People who know the why make good decisions when you are not in the room. People who only know the what wait for you, or push back in the hallway.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE IN PRACTICE
Before you hand out the new form: “Here is why. The old one loses a third of requests at the handoff.” Credit passed down in the team meeting. Blame taken first with your manager.

THE VALUE IT BRINGS
A team that trusts you tells you the truth, stays, and works through the hard weeks with you instead of around you.

THE PEOPLE WORK · A MANAGER’S CORE RESPONSIBILITIES
STANDARD 02 AND 04 · YOUR PEOPLE, YOUR NETWORK
The people work is the manager’s work.

WHAT IT IS
Hiring, onboarding, expectations, feedback, performance, time and leave, and hard conversations are the manager’s job, not PCB’s. PCB shows you how.

START THIS WEEK
Find out your HCM’s and your Engagement Consultant’s names, and how to reach them.

FOR YOU AS A MANAGER
Timecards, time off, reviews: the people work is the job, not an interruption to it. Doing it on time is how trust gets built.

WHY IT WORKS, WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE, AND WHAT IT BRINGS

WHY ADOPT IT
Nothing should wait for someone else to act. Your team gets answers from the person who knows them, and PCB spends its time on the hard cases, not the routine ones.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE IN PRACTICE
You hold the 1:1. You give the feedback the week it happened. You send the leave request the same day. You call your HCM when you are unsure, not instead of acting.

THE VALUE IT BRINGS
Problems get handled while they are small, by the person closest to them, and your team knows who its manager is.

=== Page 26 of 57 · Three things you never do (page key calls/3) ===
Three things you never do.

Each one protects the person in front of you, and it protects you. The same day means today.

FOR YOU AS A MANAGER
Three never-dos. Each one is a line that, crossed once, can cost someone their privacy, their claim or their case.

✕
Never ask for a diagnosis.
Leave is leave until the leave office says otherwise. Adjust the work and move on.
✕
Never investigate a report yourself.
Listen, write down what was said, report it to EOA the same day. Promise nothing.
✕
Never improvise pay, hiring or a performance process.
Each has a fair process and a cycle. Your HCM tells you what is possible and when.

=== SITUATION SIMULATOR (Who handles what) ===

Situation: A leave request (FMLA, medical, family)
Your first move, today: Thank them, adjust the work you can adjust this week, and send the request to the leave office the same day. Tell them you have done so and what happens next.
What it sounds like: “My doctor wants me out for three weeks.” “My dad is in and out of the hospital.” “I might need some time, I am not sure yet.”
Then: Plan coverage with the team without sharing why. Check in when they return; the return is a leave-office process too.
Who handles what: You: listen, adjust the load, route it the same day, keep their work private. | The leave office: decides eligibility (FMLA and Vanderbilt leave), paperwork, dates. FMLA requests go through Origami. | Your HCM: if you are unsure whether something counts, or how to cover the work.
Never: Never ask for a diagnosis or medical detail. Never decide eligibility yourself. Never let the request sit while you “see how it goes.”
Note: Exact leave-office contact and timelines to be confirmed by PCB.

Situation: An employee relations concern (coworker conflict, a complaint about a manager)
Your first move, today: Listen, write down what was said and when, and call your HCM the same day to agree the next step. Do not promise an outcome.
What it sounds like: “Two of my people are not speaking.” “Someone on the team says their coworker is undermining them.” “A team member complained about how another manager treats them.”
Then: If it is ordinary friction, have the conversation (clear is kind) and follow up in a week. If it is a complaint, follow the process your HCM sets out.
Who handles what: You: listen, document, keep it confidential, address plain conflict early. | Your HCM: the process for concerns, and when Employee Relations steps in. | Employee Relations: investigations and formal complaints.
Never: Never investigate a formal complaint yourself. Never discuss it with the team. Never punish someone for raising it.
Note: Employee Relations intake steps to be confirmed by PCB.

Situation: Something you must report (discrimination, harassment, sexual misconduct, Title IX)
Your first move, today: Listen, thank them, write down what was said, and report it to Equal Opportunity and Access the same day. Tell the person you have done so.
What it sounds like: “A coworker keeps commenting on her religion.” “He said something about my accent again.” “A student told me something happened at a lab party.”
Then: Keep treating the person exactly as before. Watch for retaliation from anyone and report that too.
Who handles what: You: report it the same day; support the person; protect them from retaliation. | EOA: receives the report, decides what happens next, runs any investigation. | Your HCM: if you are unsure whether something is reportable, ask; when in doubt, report.
Never: Never investigate, never ask the person to “work it out,” never wait for more evidence, never share it beyond EOA and your HCM.
Note: EOA reporting routes to be confirmed by PCB.

Situation: A performance concern
Your first move, today: Have the clear conversation first: what you expected, what you saw, what changes by when. Write it down. If it does not change, call your HCM before you do anything formal.
What it sounds like: “The work has slipped for a month.” “Deadlines keep moving.” “A talk did not fix it.”
Then: Set a check-in date. Recognize improvement when you see it. Follow the process if you do not.
Who handles what: You: clear expectations, specific feedback, a fair chance to improve, notes on each conversation. | Your HCM: the performance improvement process, documentation, and what fairness requires. | Employee Relations: formal steps, if it gets there.
Never: Never surprise someone at review time. Never improvise a warning or a plan. Never compare them to a colleague out loud.

Situation: A time-off request (vacation, a day off)
Your first move, today: Check coverage, say yes or no out loud with the reason, and approve it in Oracle.
What it sounds like: “Can I take next Friday off?” “I want the last week of December.”
Then: Note it on the team calendar so nobody is surprised.
Who handles what: You: coverage, fairness across the team, the approval. | Your HCM: only if the policy is unclear (accrual, blackout periods, carry-over).
Never: Never sit on it. Never make people feel they have to justify vacation.

Situation: A timecard question (a 52-hour week, a missed punch)
Your first move, today: Ask about the week before you approve. Fix what needs fixing in Oracle. You are responsible for your team, and accuracy is key: timesheets and payroll must be right.
What it sounds like: “The timecard shows 52 hours.” “I forgot to clock out Tuesday.”
Then: If the hours keep climbing, that is a priorities conversation, not a payroll one.
Who handles what: You: the question, the correction, the approval, and watching for a pattern. | Your HCM: overtime rules and non-exempt pay questions.
Never: Never approve without looking. Never let overtime become invisible.

Situation: A flexible work request
Your first move, today: Check the flexible work policy, think about the role and the team, and answer within the week with the reason either way.
What it sounds like: “Can I work from home on Fridays?” “Could I start at 7 and leave at 3?”
Then: Write the arrangement down. Revisit it in ninety days.
Who handles what: You: the decision, applied consistently across the team. | Your HCM: the policy, the agreement form, and equity across the unit.
Never: Never say yes to one person and no to another for the same role without a reason you could say out loud.

Situation: An accommodation request
Your first move, today: Say yes to the conversation, and send the request to EOA the same day; the accommodation process runs there.
What it sounds like: “I need a different chair for my back.” “Could I get the meeting notes in advance because of my hearing?”
Then: Check in after the accommodation is in place.
Who handles what: You: the first step of the interactive process, a supportive response, and making the accommodation work day to day. | EOA: the accommodation process, documentation, and the decision.
Never: Never ask for a diagnosis. Never decide the accommodation yourself. Never treat the request as a performance issue.

Situation: A seat opens and you want to fill it
Your first move, today: Call your HCM first. Hiring starts with a requisition and an approval chain, and the job description may need updating.
What it sounds like: “Priya gave notice.” “We finally got the budget for another analyst.”
Then: Plan the first ninety days before day one; onboarding is yours too.
Who handles what: You: the job description, the interview plan, structured questions, the decision. | Your HCM: the requisition, the approval chain, the posting, and the offer process.
Never: Never promise the job to anyone. Never interview without a structure.

Situation: You want to change someone’s pay
Your first move, today: Ask your HCM first. Pay has a process and a cycle; what is possible depends on the calendar and the case.
What it sounds like: “She took on half of a vacant role.” “He got an outside offer.”
Then: Tell the person the honest timeline.
Who handles what: You: the case, in writing: what changed and why it matters. | Your HCM: the compensation process and the merit cycle.
Never: Never hint at a raise you cannot deliver.

Situation: Someone resigns
Your first move, today: Thank them, ask about timing, and tell your HCM the same day. Then plan the handoff.
What it sounds like: “I have accepted another job.”
Then: Hold a short exit conversation yourself: what would have kept you?
Who handles what: You: the handoff plan, the team message, the last-day logistics. | Your HCM: the offboarding checklist, final pay, access removal.
Never: Never take it personally in front of them. Never let knowledge walk out undocumented.

Situation: A new hire starts Monday
Your first move, today: Block their first day and first week now: who they meet, what they read, what they do first, and the 1:1 on day one.
What it sounds like: “Their start date is next week and I have not planned anything.”
Then: A 30-day and 90-day check-in on the calendar before they arrive.
Who handles what: You: the plan, the expectations, the first thirty days. | Your HCM: Oracle setup, payroll, badge, required training.
Never: Never let day one be a laptop and silence.

Situation: A team member seems to be struggling (stress, mental health)
Your first move, today: Listen without diagnosing. Say what you can adjust. Point them to the Employee Assistance Program and, if leave comes up, the leave office.
What it sounds like: “I am not sleeping.” “Everything feels like too much right now.”
Then: Check in next week, on purpose.
Who handles what: You: the conversation, the workload, the follow-up. | The Employee Assistance Program: confidential support. | The leave office: if time away is needed.
Never: Never ask for a diagnosis. Never share it with the team. Never ignore a safety concern; if someone is at risk, call for help immediately.
Note: EAP contact to be confirmed by PCB.

Situation: An injury or a safety concern at work
Your first move, today: Get them care first. Report the injury the same day through the workplace injury process, and fix or flag the hazard.
What it sounds like: “I hurt my back moving the boxes.” “That cord has tripped two people.”
Then: Tell the team what changed.
Who handles what: You: care first, the report, the fix. | Occupational Health and Safety: the injury report and follow-up. | Your HCM: if it turns into leave or accommodation.
Never: Never wait to report. Never send someone back to an unsafe task.
Note: Injury reporting steps to be confirmed by PCB.

=== WRITTEN SITUATIONS AND RESPONSES (practice pages) ===
These situations are written for a university business office; when the learner picks another world (Campus Dining, VUIT, Academic Affairs, Student Affairs, Facilities, Development and Alumni Relations) the reports, systems, forms and people change to that unit, and the same three responses apply.

Situation (idea1): Monday, 8:05. Forty-one emails, two marked urgent, and a 1:1 with Sam at 9. The grant report your team owns is due Thursday and nobody has started it.
A. Clear the inbox first. Then the day is yours. Outcome: By ten the inbox is empty, and full again. The report is still not started and Sam’s 1:1 got moved. The sand went in first, and the big rocks did not fit.
B. Write the three things that matter this week, tell the team, then open email. [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: The report owner named by 8:15, Sam’s 1:1 kept, the vendor decision made by noon. The forty-one emails fit around them. Most were sand.
C. Start the report yourself so it is done right. Outcome: The report gets done and your week does not. The team never learns what mattered, and Sam’s 1:1 slips again.

Situation (idea2): Priya’s summary has come back twice. Both times you wrote “looks good, maybe tighten it up a bit?” It is due tomorrow and it is still not what you need.
A. “Maybe take one more pass when you get a chance.” Outcome: Third draft, same problem. Priya has now spent three days guessing what “tighten” means. Unclear is unkind.
B. “It needs the decision on page one, under three hundred words, by three tomorrow. The director only reads the first page.” [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: One draft, done by two. Priya knows what good looks like and why. The next one comes in right the first time.
C. Rewrite it yourself tonight and send it. Outcome: It ships. Priya learns nothing, and you now own every summary from here on.

Situation (safe1): Jordan tells you on Tuesday that the software vendor missed its date and the new expense system launch will slip a week. She looks braced for your reaction.
A. “Why am I only hearing about this now?” Outcome: A fair question and the wrong first sentence. Jordan learns that bad news gets a bad reaction. Next time you hear it later, or from someone else.
B. “Thank you for telling me today. What do we know, and what do you need from me?” [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: Jordan relaxes and gives you the full picture. Thank first, solve second, learn the cause later. She will bring you the next one earlier.
C. “Okay. Let me handle it from here.” Outcome: It sounds supportive, and it tells Jordan she cannot be trusted with the recovery. Ask what she needs and let her run it with your help.

Situation (idea4): Central is replacing the intake form. You have to tell the team Friday. Two people have already said “here we go again” in the hallway.
A. Forward the announcement: “FYI, new form starts Monday.” Outcome: The hallway version wins. The team hears what changed and nobody knows why, so it lands as one more thing done to them.
B. “Here is why: the old form lost a third of requests at the handoff. This fixes that. What worries you about it?” [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: Still not loved, but understood. Two worries surface that central had not thought of, and you carry them back. Two people offer to test it early.
C. “I do not love it either, but it is what it is.” Outcome: Honest, and it costs the team its manager. Nobody’s concerns go anywhere.

Situation (idea5): Dev has missed two deadlines this month. A coworker mentions it in passing. You are new, and you would rather PCB handled it.
A. Email your HCM: “Can you talk to Dev about his deadlines?” Outcome: Your HCM sends it back with one question: have you talked to him? The conversation is yours. PCB shows you how; it does not have it for you.
B. Have the conversation this week: what you noticed, what you expect, what he needs. Ask your HCM how to note it. [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: Dev was waiting on data from another team and did not know it mattered to you. Fixed in fifteen minutes. Your HCM coached you on the note in five.
C. Wait for the performance review to bring it up. Outcome: Four more months of missed deadlines, then a review that surprises him. Late feedback is wasted feedback.

Situation (task): It is Wednesday. The monthly report your team owns is due Friday. Priya, who builds it, has not mentioned it in two weeks, and you have not asked. A director just emailed you asking whether it will be on time.
A. Reply “yes” to the director, then build the report yourself Thursday night to be sure. Outcome: The report ships. You are now the person who builds it, Priya does not know she was doubted, and next month you are in the same spot. Doing the work is not the job; checking it is.
B. Reply “I will confirm today,” then ask Priya in a fifteen-minute check-in where the report stands and what she needs. [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: Priya is two-thirds done and waiting on a Finance extract she asked Dana in Finance for ten days ago. You call Dana in Finance, colleague to colleague, find out what she needs from your team, confirm Friday with the director, and put the monthly check-in on the calendar for the second week of every month so you never learn this on a Wednesday again.
C. Forward the email from the director to Priya with “Please make sure this is on time.” Outcome: Priya reads it as blame from two levels up. The report may still ship, but you have clarified nothing (what does “on time” mean, what is blocking her) and you have spent trust. Checking work is a conversation, not a forwarded email.

Situation (relations): Marcus, one of your strongest people, stays after the 1:1 and says: “My dad has been in and out of the hospital. I might need some time off for it, I am not sure yet. I did not want you to think I was slacking.”
A. “I am so sorry. Take whatever you need. Just let me know what is going on with him so I can plan around it.” Outcome: Warm, and it asks for a diagnosis, which you must never do. It also leaves a possible leave request sitting with you instead of with the leave office. The kindness is right; the routing is missing.
B. “Thank you for telling me. Let us move the two deadlines you have this week. I am going to send your situation to the leave office today so the options are ready if you need them, and nothing you told me affects how I see your work.” [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: Marcus has what he needs: the load adjusted now, the leave process started without him having to ask twice, and no request for medical detail. Helping and a duty to route arrived in the same sentence; you handled both.
C. “No problem at all. Let me know if it becomes an issue.” Outcome: Friendly, and nothing changed. The deadlines still stand, the leave request is not routed, and Marcus learns that telling you things does not help. Being pleasant is not helping.

Situation (change): Central is replacing the travel reimbursement system in six weeks. The announcement email goes out next Tuesday. Your team already hates the idea; two people have said “here we go again” in the hallway.
A. Wait for the announcement email and forward it with “FYI, let me know if you have questions.” Outcome: The team hears about it from central, not from you, and the hallway version wins. Forwarding is not explaining the why.
B. In Thursday’s team meeting, before the email, explain in your own words why the change is happening and what it fixes, ask what worries them, and write the worries down to take back to central. [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: The team still does not love it, but they heard the why from you first, their objections went somewhere, and two of them volunteer to test the new system early. Small and weekly is how uncommon agility actually happens.
C. Tell the team you also think it is a bad idea, but there is nothing anyone can do. Outcome: It feels honest and it costs the team its manager. You have joined the hallway instead of leading it, and nobody’s concerns will reach the people who could act on them.

Situation (external): A director in another unit emails you Monday: “We need your team to pull a full data reconciliation by Friday for our audit prep.” Your team’s own month-end close is the same week. Doing both is not possible without weekend work.
A. Say yes and ask the team to work the weekend. Better not to upset a director. Outcome: The work ships, the team works the weekend, and next quarter the same director asks again, sooner, because it worked. Absorbing every request is what speaking up replaces.
B. Reply the same day with the numbers: the hours the reconciliation takes, the month-end close it collides with, and two options, the part they need most by Friday with the rest the following Wednesday, or all of it with the month-end close pushed, and ask what they actually need. [the Vanderbilt way] Outcome: They needed three of the seven tables by Friday and did not know the month-end close was that week. You deliver that Thursday, the rest Wednesday, no weekend. The team saw you defend their month, politely and early.
C. Reply “We cannot do this” and copy your manager. Outcome: Your manager now owns a negotiation you could have had in one email, and the director hears “no” with no reason and no option. Speaking up for your team means the numbers and the options, not just a refusal.
