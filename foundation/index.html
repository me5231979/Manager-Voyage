    What Vanderbilt expects of a manager.

    Welcome aboard.

    Management is a job with a definition. Most managers never get one; they inherit habits from whoever managed them. This thirty-minute foundation gives every Vanderbilt manager the same definition, the same vocabulary, and the same measure, so management here is done one way. By the end, you will be able to:

    • Define management as four kinds of behavior: task, relations, change, and external
    • Name the fifteen behaviors inside them and recognize each one in a real week
    • Distinguish each behavior done the Vanderbilt way from the habit it usually replaces
    • Explain the Managerial Practices Survey you are about to receive, and what its results mean
    • Rate yourself on the fifteen behaviors and pick the one to practice first

    Same definition. Same vocabulary. Same measure. That is what a standard is.

    The belief

    A manager is the single greatest influence on a team’s engagement. Vanderbilt will not leave what management means to chance.

    The institutional why · Crescere aude

    Vanderbilt will define the great university of the 21st Century and be it.

    That is Chancellor Diermeier’s institutional vision, one sentence with no hedge in it. The how is equally blunt: operate at uncommon speed, agility, and scale to address society’s challenges through education and research, and help reclaim the essential role of leading research universities in driving American prosperity. Speed, agility, and scale are not properties of a strategy. They are properties of how 4,600 staff are managed, day to day, by you and your peers.

    Areas of Focus

    Three ways Vanderbilt delivers on the vision

    1

    Exceptional Core Operations

    With speed, agility and scale, Vanderbilt will distinguish itself with innovative and impactful capabilities to ensure that we have the talent, resources and reputation necessary to deliver on our vision.

    2

    Bold Strategic Initiatives

    With exceptional core operations as our fuel, Vanderbilt will pursue increasingly bold initiatives that enable and enhance the reach and impact of our education and research mission.

    3

    Industry Leadership

    Through strategic advocacy efforts and modeling the role of an essential research university, Vanderbilt will stimulate industry change and combat existential threats to our education and research mission.

    How this course delivers

    Exceptional Core Operations is task-oriented management done well: clear expectations, monitored work, problems solved fast. Bold Strategic Initiatives need change-oriented managers who can explain why and encourage the new idea. Industry Leadership is external behavior: managers who represent their team, watch the environment, and build the relationships the work needs. The four categories you are about to learn are the vision, at the scale of one team.

    The reputation flywheel, and your place in it

    Reputation and resources feed each other: reputation attracts talent, funding, partnerships, and access and influence, which build the reputation. The university spins that wheel with execution with ambition, values-driven leadership, and athletics momentum, and it now turns in San Francisco, West Palm Beach, New York City, and Chattanooga as well as Nashville. Execution with ambition is a team whose manager plans, clarifies, and monitors. Values-driven leadership is a manager who supports, develops, and recognizes people the same way every week.

    The framework: four categories, fifteen behaviors

    Vanderbilt uses Gary Yukl’s taxonomy of leadership behavior. Decades of research on what managers actually do, sorted into four categories by what each one is for. Read the map now; the next four segments take one category each.

    The idea

    Every category answers a different question. Task: is the work getting done well? Relations: are the people growing and staying? Change: is the team getting better at what it does? External: is the team connected to what it needs outside itself? A good manager does all four. A great manager knows which one this week needs.

    Yukl, G. (2012). Effective leadership behavior: what we know and what questions need more attention. Academy of Management Perspectives, 26(4), 66 to 85. The Managerial Practices Survey you will receive measures these same fifteen behaviors.

    Drill

    Fact or fiction

    Tap each claim to see the answer. Six things you will hear about management in any hallway on campus.

    Fiction

    Management is behavior, and behavior can be named, measured, and practiced. That is the whole premise of the framework and of this program.

    Fact

    A team with clear goals and no support burns out. A team with support and no clarity drifts. The research is consistent: effective managers do both.

    Fiction

    Doing the work is not on the list. Planning it, clarifying it, monitoring it, and solving its problems are. The most common new-manager failure is staying the best individual contributor on the team.

    Fact

    Encouraging a better way to run the weekly report is change-oriented behavior. So is a ten-minute debrief after a project. Teams that learn from experience improve without a reorganization.

    Fiction

    Every team depends on people outside it. A first-line manager who knows the right person in Finance, watches for a policy change, and speaks up for the team’s workload is doing external behavior.

    Fact

    The Managerial Practices Survey asks how often you do each behavior. Behavior can change by next month; personality cannot. That is why the program measures behavior.

    Segment 2 · Task-oriented behavior

    The four behaviors

    The idea

    Task-oriented behavior is about efficiency and reliability: the work gets done, done right, and done on time. It is the category new managers most often skip, because they assume the team knows what they know. The team does not. Flip each card for the behavior, what it looks like on a Tuesday, and where you practice it in Manager Voyage.

    The habit it replaces

    Assuming the team knows. Sending the goal in an email once. Checking the work when it is late. Fixing problems yourself because it is faster.

    The Vanderbilt way

    Goals written where the team can see them. Expectations said out loud and confirmed back. Progress checked in the 1:1 every week. Root cause found, decision made, team told.

    Segment 3 · Relations-oriented behavior

    The four behaviors

    The idea

    Relations-oriented behavior builds the trust, skill, and commitment that task behavior runs on. It is where Vanderbilt’s engagement scores are made or lost, and it is the category the cohort spends the most time rehearsing. Flip each card.

    The habit it replaces

    Being friendly instead of supportive. Waiting for the annual review to develop anyone. “Great job, team.” Delegating tasks but keeping every decision.

    The Vanderbilt way

    Listen, adjust, and route what needs routing. A growth conversation each quarter. Praise that names the person and the act. Decisions delegated with the boundary stated.

    Segment 4 · Change-oriented behavior

    The four behaviors

    The idea

    Change-oriented behavior is how a team adapts, improves, and learns. The Chancellor’s charge is uncommon speed, agility, and scale; none of those happen at the university level unless they happen at the team level, one manager at a time. This is not about big reorganizations. Most of it is small and weekly. Flip each card.

    The habit it replaces

    Forwarding the announcement. Managing the team as if next year looks like this year. “That is not how we do it here.” Moving to the next project without a look back.

    The Vanderbilt way

    The why, in your own words, before the email. A two-sentence picture of next year. “Try it and show me.” A ten-minute debrief, written down.

    Segment 5 · External behavior

    The three behaviors

    The idea

    Every team depends on information, resources, and goodwill it does not control. Vanderbilt is 39 business units, schools, central offices, and now five cities; the manager is the team’s connection to all of it. This is the category new managers most often do not know exists. Flip each card.

    The habit it replaces

    Meeting your HR partner at the first crisis. Learning about the policy change from the team. Absorbing every request so nobody is upset.

    The Vanderbilt way

    Names known before they are needed. The change read before it lands. The team’s workload defended with numbers, politely and early.

    Segment 6 · The Managerial Practices Survey

    What is coming to your inbox

    The survey

    The Managerial Practices Survey

    The survey asks how often you do each of the fifteen behaviors you just learned, on a frequency scale. It measures behavior, not personality or performance. Nobody passes or fails it. It is a map of where you already are, so the program can meet you there.

    1. It arrives by email from the Futures Learning Hub after you complete this foundation. Complete it when it arrives; it takes about fifteen minutes.
    2. Answer for a typical month, not your best week. The value of the survey is accuracy.
    3. You get the results back with your profile across the four categories and the fifteen behaviors. Your unit leader and Engagement Consultant use the same vocabulary when they talk with you about it.
    4. You take it again later in the program, so the change shows.
    How to read a result

    A low score on a behavior is not a verdict; it is a behavior you do less often than the job needs. Behaviors are the most changeable thing about a manager. The self-rating below is practice for reading your own profile, and it sets the order your micro modules open in.
