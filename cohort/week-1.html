What it means to manage.

        What are the differences between an individual contributor and a manager?

        01

        The one change

        The work you were hired for is now the team’s work. You are measured on what they produce, how they are doing, what gets better, and how the team connects to the rest of Vanderbilt. Not on what you personally finished.

        02

        Four expectations, already in force

        Get the work done through your team. Take care of your people. Make things better. Connect your team to the rest of Vanderbilt. Your assessment rated you on ten statements that sit under these four. You are not learning the standard today; you are finding it in your own week.

        03

        Why these four

        They are Gary Yukl’s four categories of effective leadership behavior: task, relations, change, and external. Fifty years of research say effective managers do all four. Most new managers do one.

        A week on {team}. Sort it into the four expectations.

        FacilitatorGroups of four, one phone per group, five minutes to enter every phrase, then Submit for analysis. Ask each group which shift they missed; it is almost always letting go or setting direction. Read the synthesis aloud. Then the sort: do two items on the screen as a room, arguing out loud, then everyone on their own phone. Ask: “Which expectation did you reach for least? Hands up if it was 04.” It is usually 04. Say so.

        Ten minutes. The next forty are the ones you will remember, so come back ready to talk out loud.

        Honest about where you are strong, and where you are growing

        Four expectations, three words each. Not modest, honest. The growing one is where this month goes.

        Not every team needs the same thing this month. Pick the expectation {team} needs most from you right now, then the one habit under it you will keep for the next four weeks. The line it writes is yours to say to the room.

        FacilitatorStrengths first, two minutes, private. Then groups of four by area if you can. Each person says their line and the others ask one question: “What would your team notice by Friday?” Collect the four expectations by show of hands; note where the room clusters. That is the month’s theme, and it goes in your notes for week four.
        Line oneWhat I noticed.The fact, with dates. Not the story about the fact.
        Line twoWhat I expect.From here, what good looks like. Said once, plainly.
        Line threeWhat I need, by when.The next step and the date. Then the question that fixes it: what is in the way?

        The Vanderbilt way: in private, the week it happened, three lines, then listen. Hinting is unkind. Handing it to HR is not managing. PCB shows you how; it does not have the conversation for you.

        Round one, in pairs

        Decide who is the manager and who is Sam. Three minutes, then swap. The cards are written for {team}; if your partner manages a different area, use theirs for their turn.

        Round two, live with Sam

        Now the real thing. Sam is a voice on your phone who answers like a person: pushback, a half-truth, a reason you did not know. Earbuds help in a full room. Sam records what you said and did not say, and you see the score.

        FacilitatorDemonstrate first with a volunteer playing Sam from the card, two minutes, and make one deliberate mistake (hint instead of saying it). Ask the room what they heard. Then pairs, timed, swap at three minutes. Then the live round: spread out, earbuds in. Debrief on the scores: “Who got ‘asked what is in the way’? Who did not? What happened instead?” Anyone who finishes early: Again, harder.
        Manager Philosophy

        What your leader gets

        Your leader has a fifteen-minute brief and one question a week. The research is plain: whether your leader asks is the biggest single predictor of whether this sticks.
