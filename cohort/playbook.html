Three rules for writing it

Write in your own voice. This is not a form for PCB; it is the document you will reread in month six. Use real names and real dates. A plan without a name and a date is a wish. Short beats complete. One true sentence per prompt is enough; the room adds the rest. Weeks two and three are capped at ninety minutes, and five of those are for this Playbook; the rest of the week is for doing it with your team.

Five time rules

Stephen R. Covey: Quadrant two first. Important and not yet urgent is where managing happens: 1:1s, goals, the look back, the conversation you are avoiding. Put those in the calendar first. The approvals fit around them; it never works the other way round.
Peter Drucker: Know thy time. For one week, write down where the hours went. Managers who do this find a third of their week was interruptions they could batch. Then batch them: approvals at one fixed time, email at three.
Andy Grove: Leverage. Some hours change the output of one person for a day. Some change the output of a team for a quarter. A 1:1, a clear expectation, a decision handed over: high leverage. Rebuilding the report yourself: low. Spend the week on the first kind.
Paul Graham: The manager’s schedule. Your day is now hour-sized blocks and other people’s calendars. Protect one half day a week with no meetings for the thinking work, and tell the team it exists so they do not book it.
FranklinCovey: Big rocks. The jar, the rocks, the sand. The week’s three rocks go in on Monday morning before the inbox opens. Everything else is sand and it still fits.

Before week one · the other half of the cohort

Your leader, and your team’s view

Two things make the practice stick: your own leader asking one question a week, and your team’s anonymous view of the same ten behaviors you rated yourself on.

Your leader: fifteen minutes before week one

  • Read the four expectations below. They are what your manager is being measured on, and what your manager’s team will be asked about.
  • Ask your manager which expectation their assessment told them to start with, and which habit they intend to keep for the four weeks.
  • Tell them the one thing you would most like to see different on their team in ninety days. Keep it to a sentence; they will write it into their Playbook.
  • Block the week-four capstone in your calendar now. Managers present their Playbook to peers and to you.

One question a week, from your leader

WeekThe questionWhy
1What is the sentence your team can expect from you? Say it to me.The Philosophy sentence. Hearing it said out loud to you is the first transfer.
2Which 1:1s are on the calendar now, and which difficult conversation did you hold?Expectation 01. If the 1:1s are not recurring holds by Friday, ask again Monday.
3What decision did you hand over, to whom, with what boundary? Did you take it back?Expectation 02. The honest answer to the last question is the one that matters.
4What will your team be able to do in ninety days that it cannot do today, and who owns it?Expectation 04 and the Team Development Plan. You will hear the answer again at the capstone; challenge one date.

Your team’s view: the ten statements, from their side

Anonymous, three or more responses, before week one and again at ninety days. Answered on a five-point scale: Rarely, Sometimes, About half the time, Usually, Almost always.

  1. My manager writes down our top priorities for the period, with a named owner for each, before the work starts.
  2. My manager tells me what I own, by when, and what a good result looks like, and puts it in writing.
  3. My manager looks at my work while it is in progress, well before it is due, so there is time to help.
  4. When I am under pressure, my manager moves a deadline or a task that same week and checks back with me.
  5. My manager thanks me for specific work, by name, within the week it happened.
  6. Before my manager changes how we work, they ask for my view and it shapes the decision.
  7. When a change is coming, my manager explains the reason in their own words before the official announcement reaches us.
  8. After a large piece of work, my manager holds a short look-back with us: what worked, what did not, what we change.
  9. My manager watches what is coming from the rest of Vanderbilt so we hear it from them first.
  10. When another office asks too much of us, my manager shows the hours, names the conflict, and offers options instead of just saying yes.
